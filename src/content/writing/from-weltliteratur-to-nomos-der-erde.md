In 1827 Goethe spoke of an approaching age of *Weltliteratur*. During the same long century, European powers divided territories, standardized maps and borders, expanded international law, and bound distant regions to metropolitan markets. Literature and politics did not discover “the world” independently. Both responded to a world being made materially connected and politically unequal.

The connection is easy to state badly. Literature did not simply reflect empire, and literary exchange cannot be reduced to colonial administration. The stronger claim is that cultural and political forms of world-making relied on some of the same routes, institutions, and classifications. Books crossed oceans on commercial ships. Translators, missionaries, colonial officials, libraries, museums, and publishers decided which texts could travel and how readers would recognize them. International law assigned territories and peoples to different legal positions. Philosophy arranged societies within histories of development.

Goethe’s *Weltliteratur*, Hegel’s *Weltgeschichte*, and Carl Schmitt’s *Nomos der Erde* name different dimensions of this process. Goethe registers literary circulation. Hegel organizes the world as a history of freedom. Schmitt insists that every legal order rests on a prior division of space. Together they pose a problem that still governs cosmopolitan and planetary politics: how can different worlds enter common relations when the institutions of relation are controlled by unequal powers?

Three terms help keep the problem clear. The **globe** is an object of calculation and logistics. A **world** is a shared horizon in which persons, objects, and actions become meaningful. The **planet** is the biophysical condition that supports human worlds and exceeds them. Empire repeatedly turns worlds into a governable globe. Nationalism answers by fortifying one world against others. Planetary politics must coordinate many worlds within the limits of one Earth.

## Goethe and the Infrastructure of World Literature

Goethe did not present *Weltliteratur* as a finished canon of the best books ever written. He used the term across conversations, letters, and essays in the 1820s to describe intensifying literary traffic and to encourage forms of international attention.[^goethe] Texts were moving among languages and national traditions with new speed. Comparison, criticism, imitation, and misunderstanding followed.

His reading reached Chinese fiction, Persian poetry, Sanskrit drama, Serbian verse, and French and English periodicals. These encounters did not give him a complete view of the world. They showed him that national literatures could no longer understand themselves in isolation.

The route taken by one Chinese novel makes the material problem visible. Peter Perring Thoms’s English translation, *Chinese Courtship*, reached Goethe through networks of printing, commerce, and imperial mediation connected to Britain’s presence in China. Recent scholarship has reconstructed these conditions in detail.[^goethe-china] The episode matters because it places Goethe’s cosmopolitan reading inside the institutions that made a text portable.

This dependence does not turn Goethe into a concealed spokesman for colonialism. It reveals a harder contradiction: emancipatory communication can use coercive infrastructure. A work may escape the intentions of an empire while travelling through its ships, languages, archives, and markets.

The contradiction persists. David Damrosch emphasizes circulation beyond a work’s culture of origin; Pascale Casanova maps unequal literary capital; Franco Moretti proposes reading systems larger than individual texts. Emily Apter and Aamir Mufti object to accounts that make translation and English-language circulation appear frictionless. B. Venkat Mani follows the “bibliomigrancy” of books through libraries, publication, and digital storage.[^world-literature]

A literary world is produced by selection. Anthologies draw borders. Translation chooses among possible equivalences. Publishers and universities decide which works become representative. The familiar statement that a book has “entered world literature” therefore raises a political question: who can admit it, and through which language must it enter?

Pheng Cheah shifts attention from movement within an already existing world to literature’s capacity for world-making. A literary work can disclose forms of life that the dominant organization of reality suppresses. Its value lies neither in offering a complete image of the globe nor in accumulating cultural difference. Literature can alter what readers perceive as a possible world.[^cheah]

Goethe’s idea stands between these two senses. *Weltliteratur* describes a market of circulating books, but it also names a practice through which national self-sufficiency becomes harder to maintain. The first sense can operate comfortably inside empire. The second may disturb it.

## Hegel and the Conversion of Geography into Time

Hegel changes the scale of the problem. In his philosophy of world history, the world is not an aggregate of peoples and events. It is the process through which spirit comes to know freedom and gives freedom institutional form.

Hegel’s universalism is more demanding than abstract sameness. Freedom becomes actual through ethical life, *Sittlichkeit*: rights, customs, institutions, and relations of recognition. The universal must take shape in particular forms of life rather than hover above them.

His philosophy of history places this insight inside a European sequence. China, India, Persia, Greece, Rome, Christianity, and the modern European state occupy different positions in a directional account of freedom. In the 1830–31 lectures, world history moves from East to West and ends in modern Europe.[^hegel-history] Hegel can acknowledge non-European civilizations while assigning them to earlier stages of a history whose principle Europe claims to understand.

This operation converts geography into time. Peoples who coexist are placed in different historical presents. Empire can then describe domination as tutelage: the colonized inhabit the same year as Europeans but are said to belong to Europe’s past.

The lecture texts require care. Hegel taught the course several times, and the familiar book was assembled after his death from manuscripts and students’ notes. Newer editions separate the courses and show changes that composite editions obscured.[^hegel-editions] The architecture nevertheless remains visible: the realization of freedom is narrated through an East-to-West sequence that privileges Europe.

Hegel also leaves resources for criticizing that privilege. If universality must pass through particularity, no state can declare its own institutions universal by fiat. If freedom requires recognition, domination fails because subjects cannot recognize themselves as co-authors of the order that binds them. The useful Hegelian question is not which people carries world spirit. It is which institutions allow people with different histories to shape common norms.

That reformulation detaches mediated universality from a single universal history. It treats universality as a disputed relation rather than a destination owned by one civilization.

## Schmitt and the Spatial Ground of Law

Carl Schmitt begins from a different premise: law never floats free of space. Before rules can be applied, land is occupied, boundaries are drawn, and jurisdictions are established. *Nomos* names this relation between spatial localization and normative order. Schmitt connects it to acts of taking, dividing, and productive use. His etymology is disputed, but the political claim is clear: every legal order presupposes a material distribution.[^schmitt-nomos]

That claim exposes what legal universalism can hide. Property law can forget seizure. Sovereignty can forget conquest. International law can describe a territorial settlement as lawful after violence has established the territory to which the law applies.

Schmitt’s account of the *jus publicum Europaeum* turns on an inside and an outside. European sovereigns recognized one another as legitimate enemies and partially limited warfare among themselves. Overseas territory remained available for colonial appropriation. Restraint in Europe depended, in Schmitt’s reconstruction, on a spatial order that did not extend equal standing to colonized peoples.

This diagnosis does not make Schmitt a decolonial thinker. His alternative to universal empire was *Großraumordnung*, a plurality of large spaces organized around regional imperial powers. Smaller peoples appear as parts of a sphere rather than equal authors of its order. Schmitt developed this argument while working as a prominent National Socialist jurist, defending the regime and supporting a German sphere of hegemony.[^schmitt-politics]

His analysis remains useful because it forces law to remember appropriation. His proposed order turns that insight into a defense of competing empires. Multipolarity can distribute power among several centers while leaving domination inside each sphere intact.

## Five Operations That Made “the World”

Goethe, Hegel, and Schmitt address literature, history, and law. Their worlds are connected by five operations.

1. **Circulation.** Books, commodities, officials, soldiers, and information move through expanding networks. Interdependence grows, but the capacity to move remains unequal.
2. **Commensuration.** Languages, religions, political systems, and literatures are compared on shared scales. Whoever designs the scale decides which differences count.
3. **Partition.** Maps, property regimes, maritime zones, and borders divide space into governable units.
4. **Temporalization.** Differences among societies are recoded as positions on a sequence from backward to advanced.
5. **Canonization.** Institutions select works to represent nations, civilizations, or humanity.

In this limited sense, a canon resembles a literary *nomos*. It distributes visibility and representative status. The analogy has limits: selecting books is not the same act as seizing land. Yet both practices arrange plural materials around authorized centers. Political orders also need narratives, museums, maps, and curricula that make their divisions appear intelligible.

Literature can register what administrative world-making omits. A map must simplify; a jurisdiction must decide. Narrative and poetry can sustain incompatible perspectives, broken temporalities, or meanings that arrive late. Literature has no automatic claim to emancipation, but its forms can preserve conflicts that a completed map suppresses.

## Reading the World against Its Completion

Edward Said shows how imperial geography enters literary form. *Orientalism* analyzes the institutions that gave European scholars authority to describe and classify “the Orient.” *Culture and Imperialism* develops contrapuntal reading: metropolitan narratives must be read alongside the colonial land, labor, and extraction placed at their margins.[^said]

Counterpoint does not replace one canon with another. It changes the relations among texts. A post-imperial curriculum cannot be produced by adding non-European works to an unchanged account of the universal. It must also alter how canonical European works are situated and read.

Hannah Arendt moves from representation to political standing. The stateless person exposes the weakness of rights said to belong to human beings as such. When citizenship disappears, institutions often disappear with it. Arendt’s “right to have rights” names membership in a political world where one’s speech and action can count.[^arendt]

Her use of *world* is institutional rather than planetary. A common world exists among plural persons through durable objects, public action, and speech. Worldlessness is the loss of a place from which one can appear before others. The refugee reveals a recurring failure of both Hegelian and Schmittian orders: states embody freedom for their members while producing people whose membership is disputed or denied.

Walter Benjamin attacks the temporal logic that lets a victorious order present itself as necessary. In “Critique of Violence,” he distinguishes law-making from law-preserving violence. Force establishes an order; the institutions of that order then use force to maintain it.[^benjamin] The distinction is not a historical sociology of colonial conquest, and it should not be made to explain every legal regime. Its value here is narrower: it shows why an accomplished distribution cannot justify itself merely by becoming law.

The theses “On the Concept of History” press the criticism further. Historicism arranges events into a causal sequence whose endpoint is the victor’s present. Benjamin’s materialist historian does not add the defeated to the same procession. He interrupts the procession and reads the cultural treasure together with the anonymous labor, coercion, and loss transmitted through it. The present is not the neutral outcome of an empty, homogeneous time. It is a moment in which an unredeemed past can make a claim.

This account complicates world history at its root. A universal history written as cumulative development tends to preserve the victor’s calendar: some societies appear advanced, others delayed, and colonial violence becomes a regrettable instrument of eventual progress. Benjamin’s *Jetztzeit*, the “now-time” charged with a past struggle, makes contemporaneity politically uneven without converting difference into backwardness. It asks what suppressed possibility becomes legible when the continuity of progress is broken.

Said reads the imperial counterpoint inside culture. Arendt asks who can appear as a political subject. Benjamin interrupts the history through which successful violence legitimizes itself. They keep “the world” open as a contested relation.

## Route, Gate, Breath

Conrad, Kafka, and Celan make three structures of world-relation perceptible: the route, the gate, and the breath.

Conrad’s fiction follows shipping companies, ports, concessions, cables, and financial interests. *Heart of Darkness* turns the Congo River into a route of extraction and a route along which the language of civilization collapses. *Nostromo* binds foreign capital to republican politics in a fictional Latin American state. Framed narrators and delayed information prevent the imperial world from appearing fully knowable.

The critique remains compromised. Chinua Achebe argued that *Heart of Darkness* uses Africa as the setting for a European crisis and denies African characters comparable linguistic presence. Said’s more ambivalent account emphasizes Conrad’s recognition of imperial falsehood and his inability to imagine a political order outside the rival empires of his time.[^conrad] Seeing through imperial ideology is not the same as seeing the colonized outside imperial optics.

Kafka’s characteristic space is the threshold. In “Before the Law,” a man spends his life waiting at a gate said to have been intended for him. In *The Trial* and *The Castle*, institutions reorganize lives while withholding the terms of membership and judgment. Authority works through delay, files, subordinate officials, and procedures that never become transparent.

Kafka wrote in German as a Jew in a predominantly Czech Prague and worked within an insurance bureaucracy. His fiction cannot be reduced to the late Habsburg administration, but that setting sharpened his attention to overlapping legal and linguistic claims. The border in Kafka is not only a line between states. It is an administrative relation reproduced inside ordinary life.

Celan writes after territorial and linguistic catastrophe. Born in Czernowitz, a city repeatedly transferred among imperial and national jurisdictions, he continued to write in German after the murder of his parents and the destruction of European Jewry. German remained tied to his mother, European poetry, and the perpetrators of extermination.[^celan]

Celan does not purify the language. Compression, fracture, neologism, and *Atemwende*, the “breath-turn,” prevent inherited fluency from covering historical rupture. A *Schibboleth* is both a word and a border test. A *Sprachgitter*, a language-grille, permits contact while preserving separation. In “The Meridian,” the poem moves toward another person without claiming possession of that person’s meaning.

The three topologies distribute the world differently. The route enables movement and extraction. The gate suspends admission. Breath sustains address when territory and language no longer offer a secure home.

## Universality after Empire

Colonial powers often presented local institutions as the destiny of humanity. Rejecting that claim does not settle the question of universality. If every norm belongs exclusively to one civilization or ontology, cross-border criticism becomes difficult. A government could defend torture, dispossession, or ecological destruction as the internal practice of a sovereign world.

The distinction we need is between an owned and an unowned universal. An owned universal issues from a center that exempts itself from reciprocal change. Others become universal by becoming more like the center. An unowned universal is produced through disputes among differently situated subjects and remains revisable by those it governs.

Césaire’s *Discourse on Colonialism* rejects Europe’s claim to represent civilization against barbarism. His argument is not that Europe betrayed an otherwise innocent ideal. Colonial rule disclosed a constitutive limit of European humanism: the colonizer could proclaim the human while training himself not to recognize colonized people as fully human. Césaire’s famous reversal of civilization and barbarism attacks a universalism that protects its own violence by placing its victims outside the circle of moral comparison.[^cesaire-fanon]

Fanon begins from the subject formed inside that contradiction. *Black Skin, White Masks* examines how colonial language and racial recognition make the colonized person seek humanity through the colonizer’s image. *The Wretched of the Earth* turns to liberation, but it does not treat the precolonial nation as a ready-made refuge. Fanon warns that a national bourgeoisie can inherit the colonial state, occupy the intermediary position once held by foreign rulers, and call this transfer independence. Decolonization must create political capacities among those who were governed, not simply replace the personnel at the center.

Césaire and Fanon therefore do more than expose European hypocrisy. They change the test of a universal claim. The question is whether those previously treated as its objects can alter its content, institutions, and language. Admission into an unchanged order is not enough.

Édouard Glissant offers another vocabulary: Relation, creolization, the *Tout-Monde*, and the right to opacity. Opacity is not isolation or an end to communication. It rejects the demand that another person or community become fully transparent inside the observer’s categories.[^glissant] Relation therefore need not culminate in absorption.

Spivak’s “planetarity” resists the globe imagined as a financial and informational object. The globe can be held before the calculating subject as a complete, traversable object. The planet, in her usage, names an alterity we inhabit but cannot master. Her proposed comparative literature begins with linguistic intimacy: slow work in languages, rhetorical forms, and singular histories, not the rapid consumption of translated difference.[^spivak] Planetarity is not a synonym for environmental scale. It disciplines the desire to possess the world cognitively.

The related language of the pluriverse emphasizes that communities make worlds through practices, institutions, and relations rather than merely occupying a common container. Arturo Escobar connects this claim to design and autonomy: technologies, landscapes, economies, and everyday arrangements can reproduce one dominant model of development or sustain collective capacities to compose another.[^escobar] The pluriverse is therefore material. It concerns who controls water, land, labor, technical systems, and the categories through which a desirable future can be imagined.

No single archive supplies “the non-Western alternative.” *Tianxia*, the Islamic *umma*, Pan-Africanism, Indigenous confederacies, Oceanic and archipelagic imaginaries, and the political projects assembled at Bandung differ in scale, membership, authority, and internal history. Some have supported egalitarian relations; some have also been recruited by states and elites. Grouping them as one civilizational answer would repeat the flattening the argument opposes.

Bandung is instructive precisely because it was neither a civilization nor a finished bloc. In 1955, representatives of twenty-nine Asian and African states and territories met amid decolonization, racial hierarchy, Cold War pressure, and sharply different political alignments. Their communique joined sovereignty and non-interference to human rights, racial equality, self-determination, and cooperation.[^bandung] Those principles did not resolve tensions among the participants, and later nonalignment often depended on state interests. Bandung nevertheless staged universality as negotiation among recently colonized peoples rather than instruction from an imperial center.

Pluriversality must be separated from multipolarity. The first concerns the irreducibility of forms of life and knowledge. The second counts major power centers. A state can invoke civilizational difference to defend its own regional *Großraum*. Several empires do not make a pluriverse.

## The Planet and the Digital *Nomos*

Ecological crisis changes the terms of the argument. Human societies inhabit different historical worlds, but they do not inhabit separate Earth systems. Dipesh Chakrabarty describes this tension as one planet and many worlds. Climate change acts through a coupled geobiological system, while responsibility and vulnerability remain divided by empire, class, race, and development.[^chakrabarty]

The planet’s physical unity does not produce a unified political subject. A common atmosphere coexists with unequal capacities to pollute, insure against damage, migrate, or recover. Planetary danger can intensify political inequality.

Achille Mbembe’s “universal right to breathe” gives this inequality a bodily measure. Written during the COVID-19 pandemic but directed beyond it, the essay joins breath to the conditions of the living: air, health, habitat, movement, and freedom from forms of abandonment that make some populations more exposed to suffocation than others.[^mbembe] Breath is universal because no life can dispense with it. Access to breathable conditions is political because extraction, policing, racial hierarchy, and environmental damage distribute exposure unevenly.

The language of breath does not replace institutions with a metaphor. It tests them. A border regime, mine, refinery, prison, or platform economy must be judged partly by whose respiration and habitat it treats as expendable. Celan’s damaged poetic breath and Mbembe’s political right belong to different arguments, but they meet at one point: a common world cannot begin from an abstract humanity while ignoring the material conditions under which particular bodies can speak and remain alive.

Technology adds another spatial order. Yuk Hui argues that political forms are also technological forms and develops technodiversity, noodiversity, and “epistemological diplomacy” as parts of planetary coexistence beyond both technological monoculture and closed civilizational blocs.[^hui]

The contemporary *nomos* is built through territory and infrastructure. Cloud systems, semiconductor supply chains, undersea cables, satellite networks, payment systems, technical standards, and intellectual property partition the planet. These systems do not abolish geography. Data centers need land, water, energy, legal jurisdiction, and physical security. Cables make some routes redundant and leave others fragile. Export controls and licensing rules turn technical components into instruments of geopolitical order.

Cultural circulation is reorganized inside the same infrastructure. Platforms determine which books become discoverable and which speech can be monetized. Digitization determines which archives can be searched, while copyright and institutional wealth determine which collections can be digitized at scale. Machine translation expands access but performs unevenly across languages with radically different quantities of standardized text. Large language models turn literary inheritance into computational infrastructure, often without preserving the context, attribution, or linguistic asymmetry of the works they absorb.

Calling this arrangement a digital *nomos* is an analytic extension, not Schmitt’s own theory. It directs attention to acts of allocation that technical language often presents as neutral: assigning addresses and standards, controlling compute, setting access rules, ranking information, and deciding whose data may be extracted. The decisive partitions may be contractual or architectural rather than lines on a map, but they still distribute the capacity to appear, move, remember, and speak.

These arrangements create a new version of the old world-literature question. Which languages become machine-legible? Whose texts enter training data? Who owns the systems that rank, translate, and reproduce them? Who can refuse inclusion, demand attribution, or correct a machine’s authoritative error? A planet divided among competing digital blocs may be multipolar and still imperial. Technodiversity matters only if communities possess a meaningful role in shaping the systems on which their political and cultural life depends.

## Conditions for a Common World

A common world need not be a single state, civilization, or language. It requires institutions through which people affected by one another can make reciprocal claims. Seven conditions follow.

### 1. Political authorship must be polycentric

Multipolarity distributes force among great powers. Polycentrism distributes authorship among states, cities, regions, Indigenous authorities, courts, scientific bodies, and transnational movements. States remain important, but neither they nor regional hegemons possess all political reality.

### 2. Rights must survive failures of nationality

Arendt’s right to have rights needs institutional form. Refugees, stateless persons, migrants, and people displaced by climate change require standing that does not vanish when citizenship fails. Layered membership, residence rights, and transnational review offer more protection than appeals to humanity without an institution to hear them.

### 3. Planetary commons cannot become a new *Landnahme*

The atmosphere, oceans, polar regions, biodiversity, orbital space, and basic knowledge systems cannot be treated as empty domains awaiting appropriation. Material distribution cannot be abolished, but those affected must be able to contest it. Future generations and nonhuman life also require forms of representation.

### 4. Translation must preserve opacity

A common world requires translation; it cannot depend on one language or epistemology. Translation should create enough mutual intelligibility for answerability without demanding total transparency. People must be able to enter institutions without surrendering every difference to administrative classification.

### 5. Universal norms must remain open to counterclaims

Human rights, ecological limits, and prohibitions on conquest cannot be reduced to optional civilizational preferences. Their interpretation also cannot remain the property of the powers that first codified them. A norm becomes universal when everyone subject to it can contest and revise its application.

### 6. Conflict must preserve the opponent's standing

A common world is not a world without conflict. Disagreement over land, memory, religion, development, and ecological sacrifice cannot be designed away. The institutional task is to prevent an adversary from becoming an existential enemy whose destruction appears to settle the dispute. Procedures matter when they protect the defeated party's capacity to return, speak, organize, and reopen a judgment. This is where Schmitt's realism about conflict must be separated from his friend-enemy criterion and imperial solution.

### 7. Historical memory must shape present distribution

Current differences in wealth, mobility, language, technology, and ecological exposure were produced through conquest, slavery, colonial extraction, and unequal industrialization. Memory matters when it changes who bears costs and who receives resources. Ceremonial acknowledgment without redistribution leaves the inherited order intact.

These conditions can be summarized as **reciprocal worldability**: no actor should have an unrestricted power to alter another’s world while denying that other the capacity to contest the relation.

## A Universal without a Proprietor

Goethe saw national literatures becoming mutually exposed. Hegel gave plurality a history of freedom but placed Europe at its summit. Schmitt recovered the spatial appropriation beneath law and answered global hegemony with regional empires.

Their concepts remain useful when read against their closures. Said reconnects metropolitan culture to colonial geography. Arendt shows that a world fails when people can be expelled from political appearance. Benjamin refuses the history in which victory proves necessity. Conrad, Kafka, and Celan give those failures a route, a gate, and a damaged language of address.

Decolonial and planetary thought add two constraints. Difference cannot license domination, and a shared Earth cannot be divided without common obligations. Neither constraint supplies a finished constitution. Together they rule out three familiar answers: government from one imperial center, withdrawal into sealed national worlds, and a balance among regional hegemons.

A non-imperial common world would still have borders, conflicts, translations, and institutions. Its borders would not erase political standing. Its conflicts would not authorize extermination. Its translations would leave room for opacity, and its institutions would remain answerable to the people whose lives they organize.

The universal is needed wherever actions cross the boundaries of a world and alter the conditions of another. It loses its claim to universality when one power reserves the authority to define it. A common world begins when that authority is shared.

On the structure and limits of the argument, see [“Carl Schmitt”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/schmitt/) in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

[^schmitt-politics]: Schmitt joined the Nazi Party in 1933, defended Hitler’s extrajudicial killings, participated in efforts to remove Jewish influence from German jurisprudence, and used *Großraum* to justify German regional hegemony. See [“Carl Schmitt”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/schmitt/), sections 1 and 4.

[^said]: Edward W. Said, *Orientalism* (Pantheon, 1978) and *Culture and Imperialism* (Knopf, 1993). Contrapuntal reading attends simultaneously to metropolitan form and the colonial histories it displaces or presupposes.

[^arendt]: Hannah Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951), especially “The Decline of the Nation-State and the End of the Rights of Man,” and *The Human Condition* (University of Chicago Press, 1958). For worldlessness, public action, and Arendt’s constitutional politics, see [“Hannah Arendt”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/arendt/) in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

[^benjamin]: Walter Benjamin, [“Critique of Violence”](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/english/Critique_of_Violence.pdf) (1921), distinguishes law-making and law-preserving violence. His “On the Concept of History” (1940) supplies the related critique of triumphal historical continuity.

[^conrad]: Chinua Achebe, “An Image of Africa: Racism in Conrad’s *Heart of Darkness*” (1977); Edward W. Said, *Culture and Imperialism*, especially the discussion of Conrad’s “overlapping territories” and “intertwined histories.”

[^celan]: Paul Celan was born in Czernowitz in 1920; his parents died during the Holocaust, and he settled in Paris after the war. See John Felstiner, *Paul Celan: Poet, Survivor, Jew* (Yale University Press, 1995), and Celan’s 1960 Büchner Prize address, “The Meridian.”

[^cesaire-fanon]: Aimé Césaire, *Discourse on Colonialism*, trans. Joan Pinkham (Monthly Review Press, 1972); Frantz Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, trans. Richard Philcox (Grove Press, 2008), and *The Wretched of the Earth*, trans. Richard Philcox (Grove Press, 2004). The paragraph distinguishes their arguments rather than treating “decolonial thought” as a single doctrine.

[^glissant]: Édouard Glissant, [*Poetics of Relation*](https://press.umich.edu/Books/P/Poetics-of-Relation2), trans. Betsy Wing (University of Michigan Press, 1997), especially “For Opacity.” Glissant’s opacity resists forced transparency while remaining part of relation.

[^spivak]: Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, [*Death of a Discipline*](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/death-of-a-discipline/9780231503235/) (Columbia University Press, 2003), especially the discussion of planetarity and a renewed comparative literature. Her planet is an ethical and epistemological figure, not simply another name for the Earth system.

[^escobar]: Arturo Escobar, [*Designs for the Pluriverse: Radical Interdependence, Autonomy, and the Making of Worlds*](https://www.dukeupress.edu/designs-for-the-pluriverse) (Duke University Press, 2018). Escobar's argument emerges from Latin American political ontology and autonomous design; it should not be generalized into a consensus among Indigenous or decolonial movements.

[^bandung]: The Asian-African Conference met in Bandung, Indonesia, from 18 to 24 April 1955 and included representatives of twenty-nine governments. On its principles and the changing historiography of nonalignment, see Eric Burton, [“A Critical Review of the Non-Aligned Movement in Contemporary Historiography”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/critical-review-of-the-nonaligned-movement-in-contemporary-historiography/0BDBF4D8D5C505A90441A3C59B58B76E), *The Historical Journal* (2026).

[^chakrabarty]: Dipesh Chakrabarty, [*One Planet, Many Worlds: The Climate Parallax*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/distributed/O/bo196813843.html) (Brandeis University Press, 2023). Chakrabarty distinguishes the planet’s geobiological unity from the political and historical plurality of human worlds.

[^mbembe]: Achille Mbembe, [“The Universal Right to Breathe”](https://doi.org/10.1086/711437), *Critical Inquiry* 47, supplement 2 (2021): S58–S62. The essay first circulated in 2020 in the context of the pandemic and links breath to a wider politics of the living.

[^hui]: Yuk Hui, [*Machine and Sovereignty: For a Planetary Thinking*](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9781517917418/machine-and-sovereignty/) (University of Minnesota Press, 2024), develops planetary politics through biodiversity, noodiversity, technodiversity, and epistemological diplomacy.
